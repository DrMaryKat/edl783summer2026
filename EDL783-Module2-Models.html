Section 1: Clinical Supervision in Depth

Clinical supervision is the most extensively studied and widely implemented supervisory model in American education. Developed in the 1960s by Morris Cogan and Robert Goldhammer at Harvard University, the clinical model was a deliberate response to the ineffective, inspection-oriented supervision that dominated schools. The term "clinical" was borrowed from medicine, reflecting the idea that supervision—like medical practice—should be data-driven, collaborative, and focused on improving outcomes through careful analysis of practice.

The Five Phases of Clinical Supervision

Pre-Observation Conference
The supervisor and teacher meet before the observation to establish the focus, context, and purpose. The teacher shares the lesson plan, identifies areas of concern or focus, and discusses the students and learning goals. This phase establishes the observation as a collaborative effort, not a surprise inspection. Effective pre-conferences build trust and ensure that the observer knows what to look for.
Observation
The supervisor observes the lesson using systematic data collection methods agreed upon during the pre-conference. Data collection might include scripting (recording dialogue verbatim), tracking patterns of questioning, mapping teacher movement, tallying student engagement behaviors, or using a focused observation instrument. The key principle is that the observer collects evidence, not judgments.
Analysis and Strategy
After the observation, the supervisor analyzes the collected data to identify patterns, strengths, and areas for growth. The supervisor plans the post-conference strategy, considering how to present the data in ways that promote teacher reflection rather than defensiveness. This phase requires skill in data interpretation and an understanding of the teacher's developmental level.
Post-Observation Conference
The supervisor and teacher meet to discuss the observation data. The most effective post-conferences are dialogic—the supervisor shares data and asks questions that help the teacher analyze their own practice. Rather than delivering a verdict, the supervisor facilitates the teacher's reflection and collaborative goal-setting. The conference should result in specific, actionable next steps.
Post-Conference Analysis
The supervisor reflects on the entire cycle—assessing both the teacher's growth and the effectiveness of their own supervisory practice. This metacognitive phase distinguishes clinical supervision from simple observation-feedback models. It ensures continuous improvement of the supervisory process itself.
Critical Insight: Research consistently shows that clinical supervision is most effective when it is implemented with fidelity—that is, when all five phases are conducted with genuine attention to collaboration and reflection. In many schools, clinical supervision has been reduced to a brief observation followed by a cursory conference, stripping the model of its transformative potential (Pajak, 2003).

Strengths and Challenges of Clinical Supervision

Clinical supervision's greatest strength is its emphasis on data and dialogue. When implemented well, it creates a structured space for teachers to examine their own practice through evidence rather than opinion. It positions the teacher as a professional partner in the supervisory process and focuses on instruction rather than personality or compliance.

However, clinical supervision is time-intensive. A single cycle—from pre-conference through post-conference analysis—can require 3-4 hours per teacher. For principals supervising 20 or more teachers, implementing full clinical supervision cycles for every teacher may be impractical. This reality has led to models of differentiated supervision that reserve intensive clinical cycles for teachers who will benefit most.

Section 2: Alternative and Synthesized Models

While clinical supervision provides a foundational framework, the field has developed numerous alternative and complementary models. Effective leaders develop fluency in multiple approaches so they can select and adapt based on context, purpose, and the needs of individual teachers.

Cognitive Coaching (Costa & Garmston)

Cognitive coaching is a non-evaluative supervisory approach focused on developing teachers' self-directedness—their capacity to monitor, analyze, and improve their own practice. Rather than providing answers, the cognitive coach asks mediative questions that help teachers examine their thinking, articulate their goals, and reflect on results. The three core conversations are the Planning Conversation, the Reflecting Conversation, and the Problem-Resolving Conversation.

Key principle: The goal is to develop the teacher's cognitive autonomy, not to transfer the coach's expertise.

Peer Coaching (Joyce & Showers)

Peer coaching pairs teachers to observe each other and provide non-evaluative feedback. Bruce Joyce and Beverly Showers found that peer coaching dramatically increased the transfer of new skills from professional development workshops into classroom practice—from about 5% transfer with training alone to over 90% with follow-up coaching. Peer coaching is most effective when both partners are learning a new strategy simultaneously.

Key principle: Observation without evaluation creates safety for risk-taking and experimentation.

Developmental Supervision (Glickman)

Glickman's developmental model recognizes that teachers operate at different levels of abstraction, commitment, and expertise. The supervisor assesses each teacher's developmental level and matches the supervisory approach accordingly: directive-informational for teachers needing clear guidance, collaborative for teachers ready to co-construct solutions, and nondirective for self-directed professionals who benefit from reflective facilitation.

Key principle: Supervision should be adapted to the teacher, not applied uniformly.

Differentiated Supervision (Glatthorn)

Glatthorn's model provides distinct tracks: clinical supervision (intensive, for teachers needing significant support), cooperative professional development (teachers working together on shared goals), self-directed development (experienced teachers pursuing their own growth plans), and administrative monitoring (brief, informal observations to ensure basic quality). Teachers may move between tracks as their needs change.

Key principle: Not all teachers need the same level or type of supervisory attention.

Artistic/Interpretive Supervision (Eisner, Garman)

Elliot Eisner and Noreen Garman challenged the dominance of technical approaches to observation. Eisner's educational connoisseurship model argues that understanding teaching requires the refined perception of a connoisseur—someone who can recognize and appreciate the subtle qualities of classroom interaction that standardized instruments miss. Garman's interpretive approach emphasizes meaning-making and dialogue.

Key principle: Teaching is an art, and supervision should honor its complexity and nuance.

Culturally Responsive Supervision

Emerging frameworks in culturally responsive supervision recognize that supervisory relationships are shaped by dynamics of race, culture, power, and identity. Supervisors must develop cultural competence, examine their own biases, and create supervisory spaces where teachers from diverse backgrounds feel valued, respected, and supported. This approach addresses systemic inequities that traditional models may overlook.

Key principle: Effective supervision requires attention to cultural context and power dynamics.

Section 3: Designing Effective Teacher Evaluation Systems

Teacher evaluation represents the summative dimension of the supervisor's work. While formative supervision focuses on growth, evaluation makes judgments about performance. Designing systems that are fair, valid, and growth-oriented remains one of the most challenging aspects of educational leadership.

Principles of Effective Evaluation

Danielson (2007) identifies several principles that should guide evaluation system design: evaluation should be based on a clear, research-based framework of effective teaching; criteria should be transparent and communicated in advance; multiple data sources should inform judgments; the process should include opportunities for teacher self-assessment; and evaluation should be connected to professional growth opportunities.

The Danielson Framework for Teaching

Charlotte Danielson's Framework for Teaching has become one of the most widely adopted evaluation frameworks in the United States. The framework identifies four domains of teaching responsibility: Domain 1 (Planning and Preparation), Domain 2 (The Classroom Environment), Domain 3 (Instruction), and Domain 4 (Professional Responsibilities). Each domain contains components rated on a four-level rubric: Unsatisfactory, Basic, Proficient, and Distinguished.

The framework's strength lies in its comprehensiveness and its emphasis on the intellectual work of teaching—not just what teachers do in the classroom, but how they plan, reflect, and contribute to the professional community. Critics note that the framework can become a rigid checklist if not implemented with professional judgment and contextual sensitivity.

Marzano's Teacher Evaluation Model

Robert Marzano's model takes a different approach, organizing 60 elements of effective teaching into four domains: Classroom Strategies and Behaviors, Planning and Preparing, Reflecting on Teaching, and Collegiality and Professionalism. Marzano's model is grounded in meta-analytic research on instructional strategies and provides detailed scales that describe teacher progression along each element.

Navigating the Evaluation-Development Tension

The most significant challenge in teacher evaluation is maintaining its integrity as both an accountability tool and a growth mechanism. Marshall (2013) advocates for "mini-observations"—frequent, brief classroom visits (5-10 minutes) that provide ongoing data without the anxiety of formal observations. This approach normalizes supervisory presence, generates more representative data, and creates regular opportunities for informal feedback conversations.

Best Practice: The most effective schools clearly separate the evaluative and developmental tracks of supervision. Teachers know when they are being evaluated and when they are receiving growth-oriented support. This clarity preserves trust while fulfilling accountability requirements.

Case Study: Selecting the Right Supervisory Approach

Scenario: Three Teachers, One Supervisor

Assistant Principal James Carter is responsible for supervising three teachers with very different profiles:

Teacher A — Ms. Rodriguez: A second-year teacher who is enthusiastic but struggling with classroom management. Her lessons are well-planned, but she has difficulty transitioning between activities and addressing disruptive behavior consistently. She is eager for help and has asked James for advice multiple times.

Teacher B — Mr. Okafor: A 12-year veteran who is highly competent and consistently rated Proficient or Distinguished on evaluations. He serves as a mentor to new teachers and leads the school's math curriculum committee. He has expressed frustration with the current evaluation system, which he views as a "time-consuming formality that tells me nothing I don't already know."

Teacher C — Mrs. Whitfield: A 20-year veteran whose teaching has become increasingly routine. Her lessons rely heavily on lecture and worksheets. Student performance data shows declining results over the past three years. She is resistant to feedback and has told colleagues that she plans to retire in five years and sees no reason to change her approach.

Guiding Questions for Analysis:

  1. Using Glickman's developmental supervision framework, at which level would you place each teacher? What supervisory approach (directive, collaborative, nondirective) would you use with each, and why?
  2. How might Glatthorn's differentiated supervision model help James create an efficient plan for supervising all three teachers?
  3. Which teacher might benefit most from cognitive coaching? From peer coaching? From clinical supervision? Justify your selections.
  4. How should James approach Mrs. Whitfield, given her resistance? What role does the principal's evaluative authority play in this situation?
  5. Consider how cultural context, identity, and power dynamics might shape each of these supervisory relationships.

Key Terms

Clinical Supervision A five-phase model (pre-conference, observation, analysis, post-conference, post-conference analysis) designed for data-driven, collaborative instructional improvement.
Cognitive Coaching A non-evaluative supervisory approach using mediative questions to develop teachers' self-directedness and reflective capacity.
Peer Coaching A collaborative model in which teachers observe each other's practice and provide non-evaluative feedback to support implementation of new strategies.
Danielson Framework A comprehensive framework organizing teaching into four domains (Planning, Environment, Instruction, Professional Responsibilities) rated on a four-level rubric.
Scripting An observation data-collection technique that involves recording classroom dialogue and events verbatim.
Mini-Observations Brief, frequent classroom visits (5-10 minutes) designed to normalize supervisory presence and generate ongoing data.
Mediative Questions Open-ended questions designed to develop a teacher's thinking rather than to direct it toward a predetermined answer.
Summative Evaluation Formal assessment that results in a performance rating and may inform employment decisions.
